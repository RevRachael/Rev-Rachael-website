Setting a Boundary Is Easy. Keeping It Is the Real Work.

I’ve gotten pretty good at setting boundaries. It took me a while to notice that setting one and actually keeping it are two different skills, and I’m better at one than the other depending on the day. Some days I draw the line beautifully in the moment, then quietly let it slide in the following week. Other days I’m terrible at drawing the line in the first place, but once I finally get there, I don’t budge.

A boundary, at its most basic, is just me saying: this is who I am, this is what upsets me, this doesn’t work for me, and here is the line I’m putting down. For example, if I’m tired, I’m going to sleep. I’ve got nothing left to give tonight. This isn’t a rejection. I’m not being cold. That’s a boundary, and it’s one of the most loving things I can do for the people in my life, because it means what I’m offering them comes from actually taking care of myself, not from having nothing left to give.

Learning myself well enough to know where those lines belong isn’t selfish. It’s the ultimate way for me to honor those around me as well as myself. As my mother used to say, I can’t give from an empty cup. By keeping myself healthy and happy, I can love deeper and help with joy instead of resentment. Boundaries allow me to protect myself without walls. It’s actually the opposite of a wall. A wall keeps people out permanently and without explanation. A boundary is designed to take care of me, on purpose, so that I have something real to bring to the people I love.

There’s a second half to this that’s easy to skip past, though, because it asks something harder of me than simply knowing my own limits. It’s respecting other people’s boundaries, even when I’m sure I know better, even when honoring theirs is inconvenient to mine.

We had a situation at the independent living home not long ago. Two women had a disagreement, and at the end of the discussion, one of them said, “I’d like to give you a hug right now. Can I do that?” The other said no. That’s a boundary. What stuck with me was that the woman who asked could have hugged her anyway. She could have decided her intention was good enough to override the answer. She didn’t. She let the no be a complete sentence, even though she believed that a hug would have helped. That’s respect for someone else’s boundary in its purest form, offered without a single word of ministry philosophy behind it, just two women at a kitchen table.

I catch myself failing this same test regularly with my daughter. She turned thirty-three this past July, and I still find myself saying some version of, “I know better, because I’m the mom, I’ve been around longer, let me tell you how to live your life, and you’re going to love it.” She is very practiced in saying, “Mom, stop.” Then it’s my job to catch myself and ask: am I respecting her boundary right now? She’s a grown woman who has been running her own life for over a decade, and even in the moments where I genuinely think she’s making the wrong call, it’s not my problem to fix. Her life is her life. Mine is mine. Loving her well means honoring where she draws her line, not proving how right I could be if she’d just listen.

This is where boundaries stopped being a self-help concept for me and started being a spiritual practice. Respecting somebody’s no when I disagree with it, when I think I have better information, when love makes me want to override it “for their own good,” is one of the more advanced things I’m asked to do. It requires me to actually believe that the other person’s authority over their own life is real, and it’s theirs, not something I get to override just because I care.

This week I’m especially focused on noticing both sides of it in my own life. Where am I holding a boundary I set and haven’t actually been maintaining, a bedtime, a phone that stays off during dinner, a limit on TV intake. Where has somebody drawn a line with me, spoken or unspoken, that I’ve been tempted to step over because I was sure I knew better.

Neither one is about control. Both are about valuing the people in my life enough to let them, and myself, be exactly who we actually are, knowing we’re each on our own path and respecting that process.

This week, take an honest look at your own two sides of it. Notice where you’re holding a boundary you’ve set with another person and haven’t actually kept, telling a friend you can’t take calls after a certain hour but picking up anyway, asking family to call first before dropping by and letting people show up unannounced instead. Notice too where somebody has drawn a line with you, spoken or unspoken, that you’ve been tempted to step over because you’re sure you know better, or because you love them enough to think your way is worth pushing.

None of this is a one-time fix. I don’t set a boundary once and get to be done with it forever. It’s a practice I return to over and over, sometimes daily, sometimes hourly, and I get better at it the same way I get better at anything: by doing it imperfectly, noticing where it slipped, and doing it again.
