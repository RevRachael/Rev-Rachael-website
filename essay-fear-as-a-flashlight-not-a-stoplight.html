Fear as a Flashlight, Not a StopLight,

A friend of mine owns a business, and a couple of weeks ago he was experiencing frustration around some incredibly repetitive tasks, the kind of tedium that feels like a waste of time. I could see exactly how to help. I thought, I could automate that. I could take that whole thing off his plate. My mouth started to open to say, “I could help you automate the process.”

Then the fear took over.

“I don’t know how to do that,” it said. “Keep your mouth shut,” it said. “Don’t make promises you can’t keep,” it said.

So I closed my mouth and walked away without saying a word.

When I got home, my David asked why I hadn’t spoken up. “Why not? Why wouldn’t you just try? You could do it.” I made some kind of noise back at him, half a laugh, half a gasp. Maybe. I don’t know. Fear, fear, fear, fear, fear seemed to be the only thing my mind would register.

Then I asked myself what, specifically, I was afraid of. Not the vague overcast, but the particulars. I named them one at a time. I’m afraid of losing face. I’m afraid I’ll get in over my head. I’m afraid of looking stupid in front of people I want to respect me. Once the fears had names, they looked smaller than they had felt.

I see him most Thursdays, so the next time we were together, I mustered up the courage to say, “Hey, I’m thinking I could create an automation program for you.” He responded with a bit of reservation, “Really?” I said, “I don’t know for sure, but I think between me and Claude, we could figure this out.” He said, “Okay, give it a try.”

Now the stakes were real. I could mess it up in front of someone whose trust I wanted to keep. I spent about twenty hours working on it, using Claude as a sounding board and a guide. I learned about programs I didn’t even know existed, worked through Twilio, hit walls, and found my way around them. The whole thing eventually came together. I understand how it works now. I learned so much, and I’m grateful for all of it, including the fear I had to walk through.

None of that would have happened if I had let that first fear win, the one that told me to say nothing. Not the twenty hours of discovery, not the new skill, not the tool I got to hand my friend that makes his job easier. The fear wasn’t wrong about my not knowing how. I really didn’t. It was wrong about what to do with not knowing.

Most of us treat fear like a stoplight. It shows up, and we stop. We read the feeling as a signal that we’ve reached the edge of what we should attempt, as if its appearance proves we’re headed somewhere we don’t belong. If I’m honest, that is usually my first response to fear.

My “not enough” story runs in the background more often than I’d like to admit: not smart enough, not educated enough, not able to get it. I’ve simply gotten good at talking myself through the untruths my old tapes like to tell.

I suppose fear can sometimes warn us of a bad situation, or tell us not to go down that street. Maybe that is more about intuition than fear. I have to check in with myself and ask, “What is this about?” A stoplight isn’t the only thing fear can be. What if it’s acting as a flashlight instead?

A flashlight doesn’t tell you to stop. It shows you the path. It illuminates the exact spot to put your attention, the very thing you’d otherwise pass by in the dark. When I named those fears that day, I wasn’t finding reasons to quit. I was finding the map forward. Each one pointed straight at a place where I could grow and gave me the chance to move toward it, not away from it.

Spiritual conversations tend to miss this about fear. We talk about releasing it, transcending it, moving past it, as though the goal is to arrive somewhere fear no longer exists. I don’t believe that’s true, and I don’t think it’s useful. Fear is part of the human experience for a reason. Science of Mind doesn’t ask me to pretend it isn’t there. It asks me to recognize the truth beneath it, that I am a capable expression of the one Power right now, mid-growth and uncertain and all. The fear can be present, and right about the difficulty, and still not get the final vote on what I do next.

I think about the difference between the woman who walked away that first day and the woman who went back on Thursday. Nothing about my actual ability had changed in between. I still didn’t know how to do it. The only thing that changed was what I decided the fear represented. Obeyed as a stoplight, it kept me quiet and safe and a little smaller than I am. Held as a flashlight, it showed me precisely where growth was waiting, and that place turned out to be within reach, one messy step and one borrowed page of Twilio documentation at a time.

This week, think of the fear that keeps returning, the one that shows up in more than one corner of your life. Instead of asking how to make it stop, ask what it is illuminating. What is it pointing you toward that you might otherwise overlook? What would it look like, starting tomorrow, if you chose to treat fear as a flashlight instead of a stoplight?

You may not feel brave when you do it. I didn’t. I was gasping a little the whole way through, half convinced I was about to prove every fear right. I walked toward what the flashlight was showing me anyway, and after twenty uncertain hours, I stepped out the other side more confident and capable than when I started.
