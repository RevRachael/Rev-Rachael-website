Knox Was Never Just One Thing Shadow Work and Wholeness

My David and I just finished watching season 3 of Silo on Apple TV, and there’s a character in that show I really like. His name is Knox, and he runs Mechanical, the crew that keeps the whole place running from the bottom levels of the silo.

Knox is a big, imposing man. He looks like the kind of guy you’d cross the street to avoid. Underneath that, though, he’s soft. He’s compassionate and caring and wise, and he’s always trying to do what’s best for the people in his department and for everyone else living in that silo.

So, I told my David how much I enjoyed Knox. I said I loved how compassionate he was. My David said he liked Knox too, just not for that reason. He liked him because when war and conflict and dissent break out, Knox isn’t afraid to step up and show the harder side of himself, the stronger side, even the violent side, as a man standing up for what he believes in. He was willing to do the hard things.

We were both right. We were watching the same character and admiring opposite halves of him, and what made Knox so compelling to both of us was that he wasn’t only one of those things. He had depth. He could be tender when tenderness was called for and fierce when fierceness was needed, and he could move between them depending on what was happening around him.

That’s what wholeness looks like to me. It’s the marriage of who we’re expected to be in society and the parts of us we’ve pushed into the shadow.

Carl Jung, who gave us the concept of the shadow in the first place, put it this way in Modern Man in Search of a Soul: “How can I be substantial if I fail to cast a shadow? I must have a dark side also if I am to be whole.”

Substantial. A person who casts no shadow isn’t a better person. They’re a thinner one, flattened out, missing a dimension. Knox is substantial because his shadow is available to him. He doesn’t pretend his fury and violence don’t exist, and he doesn’t let them run wild either. They’re integrated, so they can serve the same love that makes him gentle.

Most of us have spent our lives shying away from the “unacceptable” parts of ourselves, and humanity has been doing it for a very long time. We carry masks, and every era seems to have its own favorite one.

Think about the 1950s, or at least the picture of the 1950s we’ve inherited. Everybody had to look perfect. The perfect clothes. The perfect hair. The tidy family of Leave It to Beaver and Father Knows Best, mom and dad working together in harmony. In that picture, nobody talked about the shadows. Nobody mentioned the spousal abuse happening behind closed doors, or the alcoholism, or the arguments that went on after the guests went home. It was more important to show up as the perfect family than to be an honest one.

Jung understood exactly where that kind of hiding leads. In Psychology and Religion, he wrote, “Everyone carries a shadow, and the less it is embodied in the individual’s conscious life, the blacker and denser it is.” He explained that a flaw we’re aware of can always be corrected, while one we repress never gets corrected and “is liable to burst forth suddenly in a moment of unawareness.” The perfect family in that picture didn’t get rid of its shadows by refusing to talk about them. It only made them denser, and they came out anyway, behind closed doors, in moments nobody planned.

We’ve grown past a lot of that, and I’m grateful for it. Over the decades, people were willing to look at those shadows and say, wait a minute, this isn’t how we want to live. We want to be more authentic than this.

Then the mask simply changed shape. When social media took over in the 2000s and 2010s, the new shadow became anything less than happy. We all had to appear joyful, fun, successful, and endlessly grateful, curated down to the photo filter. Sadness, boredom, struggle, and ordinary mess went out of frame.

I think we’re moving past that mask too, at least somewhat. Still, the shadows themselves haven’t gone anywhere. The details of what we hide change with the times. The habit of hiding is much older than any one decade.

I know this in my own life. There are shadows I’ve been wrestling with for most of my years, and part of what makes them hard is that they don’t match the picture of who I’m supposed to be. Most of us know exactly which version of ourselves we’re expected to bring into a room. We bring that one, and we leave the rest at home, or in the car, or buried so deep we’ve forgotten it’s there.

Knox’s harder side isn’t the opposite of his compassion. In him, it’s in service of his compassion. His strength is what lets him protect the people he cares about. A softness with no capacity for firmness can’t protect much of anything. A hardness with no tenderness turns into cruelty. It’s the combination that makes him trustworthy, and it’s the combination that makes him whole.

Jung described this beautifully in his 1959 essay “Good and Evil in Analytical Psychology.” “To confront a person with his shadow,” he wrote, “is to show him his own light.” He said that anyone who perceives both their shadow and their light at the same time “sees himself from two sides and thus gets in the middle.” That middle is where Knox lives. It’s also what happened between my David and me that night. Each of us was looking at one side of the same man, and together, we saw him whole.

That shifts how I think about my own shadow. The parts of me I’ve been taught to hide aren’t automatically the parts of me that are wrong. Some of them are the very qualities I need in the moments that matter most. The anger that, integrated, becomes the courage to speak up. The stubbornness that, integrated, becomes the persistence to finish what I started. The fierceness that, integrated, becomes the willingness to stand between someone vulnerable and whatever is threatening them.

None of that is available to me if I’ve locked it away to keep up appearances. A shadow I refuse to acknowledge doesn’t become gentle. It becomes unavailable when I need it, or it shows up sideways when I don’t.

Wholeness doesn’t ask me to become someone different. It asks me to stop pretending to be only half of who I already am.

Knox was never just one thing. Neither am I, and neither are you.

What I am contemplating this week is which side of me gets shown to the world and which side stays out of view, whether the mask I’m wearing belongs to this moment in my life or to some older era I’ve already outgrown, and what quality in my shadow might be waiting to serve the very love I’m most proud of.
