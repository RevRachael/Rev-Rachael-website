The Man Who Was Afraid to Die: Shadow Work

Several years ago, as a hospice chaplain, I worked with a gentleman who was terrified of dying. In our world, we’d call him “at risk.” His fear overshadowed any peace he might have had in his final weeks.

So I did what I usually do. I started asking questions. Starting with the basics, I asked him why he was so afraid. With tears staining his cheeks, he told me, “Because I’m a horrible man.”

My next question was what made him a horrible man. He explained that when his children were young, he would beat them. He carried so much guilt and horror inside him, so much remorse.

Some people would respond to that with some version of “shame on you.” I understand why someone would react that way to such a story. He hurt the children he was supposed to protect. Judgment was the last thing he needed from me, though. He already had more of it than he could carry, and it was the very thing standing between him and any kind of peace.

Instead, I got curious. Why had that seemed okay to him back then? What made him pick up his belt?

His answer came slowly, through more sobs. His father had been an alcoholic, and when he was drunk, he beat this man and his siblings. He told me that to escape, he left his sisters and brother behind and joined the army as soon as he was able. His trauma was never addressed. Then, when he became a father himself, he repeated the pattern. He drank, and when he got upset or frustrated with his children, he did the same thing that had been done to him. He beat them.

It’s such a typical story of family trauma, the repetition of patterns when healing is never addressed.

It took weeks. I went back and visited him several times, and each time, he told the story again, and each time it went a little deeper. He cried. He sobbed. He couldn’t understand how his father could have done that to him and his siblings. Even more painful, he couldn’t understand how he could have created that same pain for the children he loved so dearly.

Eventually, I asked him whether he might be able to forgive his father. Over time, he got to the point where he could say, “I don’t agree, but I can forgive him.” He released the anguish he had been carrying toward his father for a lifetime, and once he could do that, something else opened. He was able to forgive himself too.

He was a Christian man, and in that process, he came to understand that God had forgiven him as well. The prayer he had known his whole life finally made sense to him from the inside: “Forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us.” The words were his, the language was his, and the peace he found was real.

Within a couple of weeks of that, he passed. He left this world in a place of peace, the very peace that had seemed so far out of reach when we first met.

When I look back on the time we spent together, I can see the process we teach at the center unfolding in him almost step by step, even though he had never heard of it.

It began with awareness. Just through our conversations, he became aware of what had caused him to do those horrific things. He saw how it came about. He saw his father, and he saw the continuation of the pattern his father had handed down to him.

Awareness took him to radical responsibility. He didn’t hide behind his father’s example. He owned his own actions. Even though he had learned it from a man whose behavior he came to hate, he was the one who made the choices, and he took full responsibility for what he had done and the environment he had created for his children.

From there, he worked from the inside out. He started looking at the whys and the hows inside himself. He forgave his father from the inside. He forgave himself from the inside. Nothing about his outer circumstances changed in those weeks, yet everything about his inner world was transformed.

One of the beautiful things about looking at the shadows in our lives is that once we see them, we can never not see them again. We can never again fail to understand how they brought us to who we are. Once a shadow is acknowledged, once it’s been brought out into the light and looked at honestly, it no longer has to be a source of shame. It becomes part of the story of how we became whole.

He did that. In his final weeks, he unshadowed the darkest part of his life.

Nothing about his healing erased what his children lived through, and his peace didn’t require them to forgive him. Their healing belongs to them. What he did was refuse to leave the world still carrying the pattern unexamined. He took his part of it out of the dark.

I think about how different those weeks might have been if I had walked into his room with judgment. He would have heard what he already believed about himself, that he was a horrible man, and he likely would have died believing it. Curiosity gave him room. A question gave him a door. He did all the walking himself.

That’s the real work of dancing with the shadow. We honor the parts of ourselves we were ashamed of, not by excusing them, but by understanding them well enough to stop being ruled by them. By doing that inner work, we aren’t only helping ourselves. We’re healing the One Mind, the whole we all belong to.

So this week, think about a part of your own story you’ve only ever looked at with shame. What would it be like to look at it with curiosity instead? Ask where it came from, how it got passed to you, and what responsibility is genuinely yours to take. Then ask what forgiveness might be available, for someone else and for yourself.
