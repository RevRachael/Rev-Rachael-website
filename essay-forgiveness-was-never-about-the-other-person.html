Forgiveness Was Never About the Other Person

In an ideal world, I would never step over the boundaries of someone I love, and they would never step over mine. We don’t live in that world. The reality is it happens all the time, in small ways and large ones. What determines whether a relationship can hold that weight isn’t whether the crossing happens, it’s what we do after.

Ideally, I would notice when someone crosses a line with me, and because they value me, they would say they’re sorry, and we would move on. Some people genuinely struggle to say those words. There are other ways to apologize besides saying them out loud, even though the words themselves still make a difference when they come. That give and take, the noticing and the owning, is part of how two people keep loving each other honestly over time.

Forgiveness gets complicated fast when the responsibility doesn’t come, and that’s exactly where most of us get stuck. Yes, it’s about the apology, but it’s also about someone taking responsibility for stepping over the boundary in the first place.

I was talking with my brother-in-law recently about tools for forgiveness. I mentioned one of the classics, writing a letter. He asked the obvious follow-up. What if you don’t mail it? What if the person it’s addressed to never reads it, or reads it and rejects everything in it? He wasn’t being difficult. He genuinely couldn’t see the point of writing something with no guarantee of a response.

I told him it was never about that. It was never about the other person receiving anything. It’s about me writing it. Forgiveness happens inside of me. When I’m upset and frustrated, that feeling doesn’t reach the other person at all. It just sits in me. If I’m hurt because someone was disrespectful and they don’t see it that way, I can carry the anguish and the resentment around inside myself, or I can find a way to release it. Writing the letter is one of the ways I release it. That’s where the healing actually has to take place, because that’s the only place I have any real control.

Would it help if the person who hurt me looked me in the eye and said, “You’re right, I was acting like a jerk, I’m sorry”? Of course it would. That’s the easy version, the ideal one, the one I’d really like to see happen. I get to be right, everyone likes being right, and I can close the loop and move on. What happens when that same person looks at me and says, “No, I wasn’t out of line, I was in the right”? Now I don’t get to be right, at least not out loud. I might still believe it in my own head, but if I need their agreement to heal, I’ve handed them a key to my own peace, one most of them don’t even know they’re holding. So I have to take it back myself. I have to go deeper. I have to ask why I’m still feeling this way and figure out how to work through it myself, because waiting on their admission isn’t a strategy. It keeps me in a victim space instead of an empowered one.

I think about my father here. When I was about twelve or thirteen, he fell into alcoholism and disappeared from my life for a stretch of years. He came back around when I was eighteen, having become active in AA, and he asked me to forgive him. I remember exactly how I felt. I was still angry and hurt, so I said, “No, I don’t forgive you,” and as an eighteen-year-old, I had every right to do that, because I was honoring my feelings.

Notice what he did next, because it’s the part of the story that actually taught me something. He didn’t collapse into needing my forgiveness on the spot. He didn’t beg, or make his continued sobriety somehow contingent on my saying the words. He kept doing his own work. He did his penance in whatever form that took for him, and he dealt with the wounds he needed to deal with. Eventually he forgave himself, because his healing was never about me. It was about him facing what he had done and doing the work that came after facing it. In time, I did come around, but his healing didn’t wait for my timeline, and honestly, mine shouldn’t have to wait for his.

I think about a patient I sat with recently, a woman in her late seventies telling me the story of her life. She said she never understood why her mother had been so cruel to her after she got pregnant, unmarried, at eighteen. What made it sting worse was that her own mother had gotten pregnant at fifteen, also unmarried. She’d expected compassion from someone who had lived the exact same story. Instead she got cruelty.

I said, hurt people hurt people. When we don’t take the time to heal our own wounds, we genuinely don’t know how to respond with anything other than what was done to us. It isn’t an excuse. It’s understanding the motivation, not excusing the behavior. It’s just about the truth of where cruelty comes from.

So if you’re carrying something right now, waiting for someone to say the words that would make it easier to let go, do the harder and more freeing thing instead. Write the letter. You don’t have to mail it if you don’t want to. Write it because you need to say it, to put it into words, not because you need to be heard. It was never about them hearing it at all. The apology you’re owed may never come, and you get to heal regardless. It’s time to stop letting them have free rent in your head.

I would ask you to take the time this week to think of the person you’re still waiting to hear from, the apology that hasn’t come and might never come. Ask yourself honestly, whose peace am I actually waiting on right now, theirs or mine? Then write the letter, not to send it. Write it to come to your own place of acceptance and release, not because what they did was okay, but because you get to release the anger and the hostility you’ve been carrying, and let it go.
