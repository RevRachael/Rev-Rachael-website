The Clarity That Only Struggle Could Give

I remember exactly where I was sitting. My David was in the garage working on the car, and I was in the door jamb between the garage and the foyer, the cold air hitting my front and the hot air hitting my back. I was crying. “I can’t do this. It’s too hard. I don’t want to do this anymore.”

I was in the middle of graduate school at the time, and my dyslexic brain was making reading, processing, and writing information extremely difficult. The assignments that seemed to come easily to the people around me took me two or three times the effort and time. On that particular day, I had hit a wall, and it felt like it was simply too hard to keep going.

My David didn’t try to fix it or talk me out of the feeling. He just said, “But you’re going to.”

“I know I’m going to,” I told him. This was not a new experience for me. Even though everything inside me in that moment wanted to give up, I wasn’t actually going to quit. I wouldn’t have been able to live with myself if I did. I know myself well enough to know I will get back up and try again.

I have had to get up again, and again, and again, because academics are a struggle for me. Brain imaging research has shown that a dyslexic brain decodes words through slower, more effortful pathways than a typical reader’s brain, so the same page simply costs me more to get through. I went over the same material more times than I want to count, not because the ideas were beyond me, but because getting the information into my brain in the first place took that many passes. Through that struggle, and I mean specifically through it, not around it or despite it, I developed something I don’t think I would have found any other way. I found the strength and the knowing to do whatever gets put in front of me, when it’s terrifying and when it doesn’t come easily.

I don’t think that strength shows up if the struggle doesn’t. That idea goes against what most of us are taught. We learn that peace is the destination and struggle is just a detour we’re supposed to get through, or around, as fast as we can. Avoiding the grappling does a disservice to true growth and depth of character. Think of a sapling raised inside a wall built to protect it. It never has to fight the wind, so its roots never go deep, and the day that wall comes down, the first real gust knocks it flat. A tree that grows out in the open has to push against the wind its whole life, and every storm it survives sinks its roots a little deeper, until it can stand through almost anything. We need the same push to grow the same kind of strength. I found my true strength by going through the struggle and out the other side of it.

So what does the grappling force me to face in myself that I would normally look away from? Sitting in that doorway, it forced me to face the fact that I couldn’t do school the way most people seemed to be doing it. I was going to have to find my own path and different strategies, ones that would be slower and harder won than I wanted them to be. That was an uncomfortable thing to face. Still, it would have been more difficult and exhausting to keep pretending I just needed to try harder, using methods others had laid out for me. What was easier, once I let myself see it, was to stop struggling against who I am and move into the way I actually am. Instead of sitting for hours trying to make the words on a page hold still for my eyes, I found Audible and started listening instead, taking my notes off the audio rather than off the page, so I could process the information the way my brain actually processes it. Instead of writing papers by forcing myself onto the page word by word, I learned to use a dictation program, saying what I meant out loud and then going back afterward to massage it into the shape it needed. Figuring out my own way took its own kind of work. It meant I stopped rejecting the way my mind actually works, though, and started honoring the divergent mind I’ve been blessed with instead.

I think reaching for peace as proof we’re doing life right is where some spiritual practice loses its practicality. Some clarity only arrives through friction. Diamonds form under pressure, and muscles only build strength through use. Human durability is forged the same way, not despite the difficulty, but because of it. If I had never sat in that doorway crying, never gotten back up the next day to do it again, I would not have the endurance I have now. I built it out of the actual material of the hardest parts of graduate school, one hard-won pass at a time.

I certainly don’t want to imply that I go looking for struggle, that I enjoy pain, or that I should stop trying to ease it when I can. Pain does its own good work. It tells me when something is wrong, and it shows me where I need to pay attention. That doesn’t mean I like it. What it means is that when struggle shows up uninvited, and it will, I’ve stopped assuming it means I’m doing something wrong. Sometimes it means I’m exactly at the edge of what I need to learn about myself. Sometimes it means there’s a part of me that needs healing.

What I am contemplating this week is the times when clarity came to me, not through peace, but through struggle. What did that discomfort force me to face about myself that I would otherwise have avoided? What did I learn on the other side of it, the strength or the truth that was only available because I went through, and not around?

You may find, like I did sitting in that doorway, that the version of you who gets back up the next day, and the day after, doing the same hard work of getting it to stick, is the one building actual strength.

“But you’re going to.” Some days that’s the whole prayer. I know I’m going to.
