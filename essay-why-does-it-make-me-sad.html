Why Does It Make Me Sad?

Why? That’s the question. I want to be clear about what I mean by this.

Why is not “why does this happen to me.” Why is not “why do people treat me poorly.” Those questions keep me pointed outward, at everyone else’s behavior, at a world I can’t control. The question that actually helps me move forward is simple, but much harder. Why does this particular situation make me sad? Why does it make me angry? Why does this trigger the emotional response that it triggers in me?

That shift, from what they did to why it affects me the way it did, is my responsibility. Not theirs. This sometimes takes me a while, finding the string to pull for a real answer. When I feel the emotion bubbling up, I know I’ve found the thought that is triggering the reaction. Typically, the thoughts come from the idea of “this isn’t fair,” “this could happen to me,” or “I feel out of control.”

I can feel angry about something and be completely right that there’s a real injustice behind it. I’m not asking anyone to pretend otherwise. Once I’ve named the injustice, though, there’s a second question waiting right behind it: why does it make me angry? What is mine to do about it? Where, specifically, is my healing in this? If I am a microcosm of the macrocosm, then what is happening out there is happening in here. If it has my attention, there is something for me to look at. What is mine to do?

David and I were driving into St. George, and he was telling me about a man who left a career in federal law enforcement to run undercover rescue operations in some of the most dangerous corners of the world, places shaped by conflict and organized crime, pulling children out of trafficking rings. I said, “Wow, that’s really impressive.” I am grateful for those who are called to do big things like that. It is not, however, mine to do. I’m guessing it’s probably not yours either. That man had a calling, though, and it takes an extraordinary kind of motivation to walk toward that kind of danger for children he’s never met, to stand against people who are, by any reasonable guess, not going to welcome him.

So if that isn’t mine, what is?

Sometimes it’s small. Helping someone cross the street. Smiling at a tired cashier. Pausing for thirty seconds with someone having a hard moment. But sometimes it’s bigger than I ever plan for, and I don’t get a say in when.

I was at a retreat in Northern California years ago, and on the drive down the mountain, I caught a ride with another man from the retreat. A motorcycle passed us, driving erratically, recklessly, clearly unsafe. I remember praying he’d get where he was going safely. He didn’t. We came around the next bend, and there he was. He’d collided with another rider, and they were both down. The man he’d hit was lying in the road with his leg badly broken, the skin and the fabric of his pants melded together, the bone showing.

I don’t remember deciding anything. I just got out of the car, walked over, sat down on the hot summer asphalt, and put his head in my lap. My leg was burning against the pavement. It didn’t matter. I stayed there, talking to him, keeping him with me, until the ambulance came. At some point I looked up and the sun had stopped hitting us. Someone I never saw again was standing over the two of us, holding an umbrella. When the paramedics finally lifted him onto the gurney, he looked at me and said, “You are my angel today.”

I didn’t plan that. I didn’t have time to ask what was mine to do. I just did it.

Most of what unsettles me is really about not knowing what’s mine to carry. Take on more than is mine, and I drown in it. Take on nothing, go numb to protect myself, and I’m still drowning, just quietly. I’ve come to believe that when we’re settled in who we are, we show up the way we’re meant to without having to think it through first, we simply know. It’s when we’re not living in our own authenticity that we can’t hear which one is actually ours to carry.

Building that kind of authenticity means looking at all of me, including the parts of myself I don’t particularly like. Here’s what I’ve come to believe: the parts of me I don’t like are just as valuable to me as the parts I do like. Not because I should pretend they’re virtues, but because they show me exactly where I need to add more light in my own life. They’re not evidence against me, but a map.

That’s a different way of relating to my own flaws than the one society taught me: hide the parts I don’t like, manage them, apologize for them, or perform their opposite in public. If I get short with someone I love, that’s not just a moment to feel bad about. That’s information. Something in me is tired, or afraid, or hasn’t healed yet, and it just told me something. I can thank it for the data instead of only punishing myself for the behavior.

This is the piece so many spiritual conversations skip past, because it’s easier to talk about gratitude and the parts of me that already shine, and a whole life includes the unlit areas too. Science of Mind teaches that we are each a perfect expression of the one Power, right now, exactly as we are, mid-growth and all. That doesn’t mean everything I do is productive. It means my worth was never up for negotiation while I do the work of becoming more conscious. The growth and the worth are two different conversations, and confusing them is what makes it scary to look honestly at myself in the first place.

I struggle with this too, caught between what I want the world to look like and what it actually looks like. So I try to remind myself: notice what’s happening inside me and what’s happening around me, and when something strikes me, when I’m fearful or angry or sad, look at what’s causing it, because the cause is where the healing is. That’s what frees me to do what’s mine.

So here’s what I’d ask you to sit with this week. Next time something stings, whether it’s a stranger’s rudeness or a comment from someone you love, resist the pull to stop at “how could they.” Ask the harder question instead. Why did I react the way I did? What does my reaction want to show me? Then ask the question that actually belongs to you: given everything on my plate, given my particular life, what is mine to do here?

You may find, like I have, that it’s rarely the dramatic rescue you imagined and much more often the ordinary kindness right in front of you. Every once in a while, it’s neither ordinary nor imagined at all, just a moment where you stop thinking and show up as yourself, imperfections and all, exactly like I am.

That’s not a smaller calling. It might be the one holding everything else together.
