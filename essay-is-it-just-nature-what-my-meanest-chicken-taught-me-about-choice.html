Is It Just Nature? What My Meanest Chicken Taught Me About Choice

I have chickens. If you don’t already know this about me, now you do. It turns out they’ve taught me more theology than I expected from a flock of birds.

We’ve raised three separate groups over time. The first four came to us fully grown. The second group came as awkward chicken teenagers, three of them. One was black and white and the runt of that little trio, so we called her Kruella. Kruella had it rough. It was the first four, the ones already fully grown when she arrived, who picked on her constantly. She was skittish, always the first to bolt, and if I walked into the coop she’d run and hide, because she’d learned that showing up visibly usually meant getting pecked at. I tried to intervene where I could, but you know how it goes with a pecking order. It has its own logic, and it doesn’t care much what I think about fairness.

Kruella was about ten months old, eleven maybe, by the time our third batch arrived, ten new baby chicks, too many to name. We raised them in the library, which was inside the condo, until they were big enough that we could merge them with the rest of the flock. We did it the way the professionals tell you to, slowly, with all the usual precautions people give you for blending a flock. I’ll tell you, Kruella, the chicken who had spent her whole adolescence being bullied and terrorized, always hiding in her own chicken coop, turned out to be the single meanest bird toward those new chicks. She’d chase them, peck at them, corner them, until they learned to flee the second they saw her coming. The bullied became, without any hesitation or self-reflection, the bully.

Now that the new chicks have grown past her in size, she’s eased off some, but I still catch her every now and then throwing a little aggression their way out of pure habit, and I still watch them scatter from a bird who could no longer actually win that fight.

Watching it play out, I found myself asking a question. Is that just nature? Is it simply built into a living thing to treat others the way it was treated? If I’d been raised in a harsh environment, would I default to harshness myself? The honest answer, at least from what I’ve watched play out in that coop, is yes, at least as a default setting. That does seem to be how it goes without intervention.

What makes me different from Kruella, though, is the part I continue to contemplate. Kruella cannot look inward. She cannot pause mid-peck and ask herself whether this is really who she wants to be, whether the treatment she received has to be the treatment she gives, because she’s a chicken. That capacity simply isn’t available to her, and I say that with real affection for her, not judgment.

I have that capacity. It is, honestly, one of the more remarkable things about being human. I get to look at the pattern I inherited, whatever it was, however it was passed to me, and ask an actual question about it. Do I want to be like this? Do I want to keep passing forward what was done to me? The freeing part is that maybe the answer is yes for now, and that’s honest, and it’s allowed, but maybe the answer is no, and if it’s no, I get to change it. It doesn’t happen instantly, and it doesn’t happen without real effort, since it takes genuine, ongoing work to notice an inherited pattern in the middle of living it and choose something different in that exact moment. If the only place I can catch it right now is a full day later, replaying what happened and wishing I’d done it differently, that’s fine. That’s actually where most of us start. The practice is what closes the gap over time, so eventually it’s three hours later instead of a full day, and eventually closer still, until every once in a while I actually catch it in the moment itself. It’s a process, not a switch, and I try not to expect myself to catch it every time right from the beginning, because that isn’t how it actually works. It is possible, though. I’ve watched people do it. I’ve had to do it myself.

This is where the whole conversation about hurt people hurting people actually goes somewhere useful, instead of just becoming a tidy phrase I hear used to explain away someone’s cruelty. It explains where the behavior came from. It does not obligate anyone to keep repeating it. The explaining and the choosing are two separate steps, and the second one is the only one that actually belongs to me.

I sat not long ago with a hospice patient who was working through her own version of this, an inherited pattern of cruelty passed down through her family that she never fully understood until she was much older. What I found interesting wasn’t that the pattern existed. Patterns like that are everywhere once you start looking. What I found interesting was how much lighter she seemed the moment she stopped needing to understand exactly why it happened and simply decided she wasn’t going to be the one to hand it forward again.

That’s the move available to me that isn’t available to Kruella. Not perfect understanding. Just the decision, the action to do something different.

When I notice a pattern in myself right now, something I picked up before I had any say in learning it, I try to do the one thing Kruella never gets the chance to do. Stop mid-motion. Notice it happening. Ask, honestly, whether it’s still who I want to be.

You get to answer that question too, just like I do, and that choice is what decides whether the pattern gets handed forward again or finally stops with you.
